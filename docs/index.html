Differential Gene Expression

The difference in expression of a gene between samples or cases. Differential expression is typically thought of as a difference in abundance or amount of expression, but may also be a difference in what cell types a gene is expressed in or where a gene is expressed spatially within a tissue.

Spatial Transcriptomic methods produce data that allow for gene expression to be investigated and described within a spatial context.

Spatial Transcriptomics

Spatial Transcriptomics (ST) refers to molecular methods used to measure gene expression at or near single-cell resolution, reporting where observed transcripts are physically located within the source tissue, preserving spatial context.

In this way, researchers can explore where a gene is expressed within a tissue and how that may change over time, based on experimental conditions, or for different clinical contexts. Spatial transcriptomics is a rapidly-evolving area of development that can be used for for a variety of biological questions such as describing cell neighborhoods, cell-cell interactions, and discovering biomarkers or drug targets.

Platforms or technologies to generate spatial transcriptomics (ST) data are typically categorized as either or . Both types will provide researchers with spatially-informed gene expression data, however one type allows researchers to investigate the whole transcriptome while the other is limited to a specified set of genes called a . Inversely, the ones that investigate the whole typically have to deconvolve to estimate single-cell data while the ones that investigate a limited set of genes have a single-cell or sub-cellular view of expression and instead must segment the data into discrete single cells (Lim et al. 2025). Often researchers will use complementary spatial transcriptomic approaches. For example, Kwon et al. The technologies and their trade-offs as well as the biological concepts (e.g., SCZ and dlPFC) will be described in more detail in the Context section of the case study.

Context

This section will cover context related to spatially-informed gene expression data as well as the human nervous system and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and schizophrenia.

What is spatially-informed gene expression data?

Spatially-informed gene expression data goes beyond just gene expression and reporting how much a gene is expressed in a sample or a cell. In addition to that, spatially-informed gene expression data anchors those “counts” to a physical location in the sample such that the location of cells is preserved and the contextual environment or neighborhood can also be considered.

Spatially-informed gene expression data is also known as spatial transcriptomics or ST data. It may also be called spatially-resolved transcriptomics or SRT data by others. It is complex, sparse, and can be noisy (e.g., due to technical artifacts associated with image-based data).

ST data vs bulk and single-cell data

Bulk RNA-seq data has very few similarities to ST data. They both provide researchers with gene expression counts. Depending on the method used for sample preparation, they both may be targeting polyadenylated tails, however for Bulk RNA-seq this would be occurring during sample preparation, while for sequencing-based ST methods, this would be occurring post sample preparation. Some ST methods don’t provide high cellular resolution and may be producing data for multicellular spots that contain 10s to 100s of cells. These methods must deconvolve the signal to separate out cells from each other; however, bulk RNA-seq is producing data for many more cells at a given time. Coverage is much higher for bulk RNA-seq than spatial transcriptomics which returns sparse, low-coverage data.

Single-cell RNA-seq data shares some more similarities with ST data, but is still quite different since Single-cell RNA-seq data cannot preserve the spatial context. ST data can be single-cellular, but it may also be sub-cellular or multicellular depending on the technology used. But the final data product will use segmentation or deconvolution to provide single-cell like data. Both utilize (single-cell) or may utilize (ST) unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to identify unique source molecules, and produce data that are sparse with low coverage.

Spatial data is the most complex of the three because it’s not just reporting gene counts, but also reporting where expression of those genes were observed in relation to other genes and other areas where that gene was expressed.

ST technologies

The types of technology used to generate spatial transcriptomics data are generally categorized as or and lie across two inverse spectra: Molecular and Cellular Resolution. Sequencing-based technologies typically provide a whole view (broad molecular resolution), but not at a single-cell or sub-cellular resolution – instead at a Region of Interest (ROI) or resolution (where a “spot” is made up of tens to hundreds of cells.) In other words, sequencing-based technologies provide low precision / cellular resolution. In contrast, image-based technologies typically provide data for a panel-based subset or a limited selection of the transcriptome (in other words, narrow molecular resolution). However, image-based technologies provide data at a single-cell or even sub-cellular resolution (high precision cellular resolution). Due to this, researchers may first collect spatially-informed gene expression data with a sequencing-based technology for initial hypothesis generation to identify a specific region of interest in a tissue or specific genes to probe where greater cellular resolution would be beneficial, and then follow-up with generating a higher cellular resolution spatially-informed dataset using an image-based technology. Kwon et al. (2026) is a study posted to bioRxiv recently where the researchers did just this: first using a sequencing-based technology followed by an image-based technology as complementary spatial transcriptomic approaches to study the impacts of within the , microenvironments, and cell types of from the human .

Broadly, how is the data generated?

How sequencing-based data is generated

Generally, sequencing-based methods use special slides that have spatially-barcoded RNA-binding probes attached to spots, with each spot having a unique spatial barcode. The probes also have a random molecular tag (UMI), and a polyT sequence to bind polyA tails of mRNA. Tissue is permeabilized after being placed on the slides such that mRNA molecules will diffuse through the tissue to the probes on the slide. The bound RNA is converted into cDNA with second strand synthesis on the slide, which is then extracted, library prepped, and sequenced off slide. Due to the spatial barcode, its original spatial location is preserved. Since this approach utilizes spots on a slide, these spots may align with multiple cells at a time rather than corresponding to a single cell. Different sized slide spots will have different cellular resolution. By utilizing 2 micron adjoining spots instead of 55 micron separated spots, Visium HD has finer cellular resolution and provides closer to single-cell results, however fewer UMIs and genes will be observed due to capture efficiency limitations and the smaller spot area.

Lim et al. (2025) notes:

Platforms like Visium HD and Stereo-seq can detect only a few hundred to a thousand genes when data are exported to achieve single-cell scale/resolution. This level of resolution comes at the expense of a reduced number of detectable genes. As of now, sequencing-based spatial technologies are not yet capable of delivering whole-transcriptome data at true single-cell resolution.

How image-based is generated

Generally, image-based methods also use special slides, however they do not have spots or reagents attached to the slide. Instead, the slides are optimized for tissue adherence and imaging protocols. Samples are mounted to the slides and any necessary reagents for in situ synthesis and hybridization are applied to the entire sample section through fluidic wash channels. Probes which are specific to certain mRNA molecules are applied to the sample and hybridize to the mRNA molecules. Secondary probes with fluorophores are then applied to the sample and imaging occurs and fluorophores are cleaved or extinguished/photobleached before the cycle repeats. Rather than one fluorophore being used to represent one gene, combinations of fluorophores across imaging rounds, (an “optical barcode”) represent a given gene. Therefore, gene-specific fluorescent signals are used to ascertain what genes are expressed, noting their spatial location within the sample.

While image-based methods achieve single- or even sub-cellular resolution, they are assays targeting a specific, predefined gene panel, limited in the number of unique genes that can be visualized.

GEO is an international public repository that archives and freely distributes microarray, next-generation sequencing, and other forms of high-throughput functional genomics data submitted by the research community.

GEO records are organized by “Platform”, “Samples”, and “Series”.

  • A Platform record describes the technology or platform that was used to generate the data. For a study like Kwon et al. (2026), two different Platform Records exists: one for the Visium Data and one for the Xenium data. A Sample record describes the conditions under which an individual sample was handled and the data that was generated from it.
  • A Sample Record can only refer to one Platform Record. Even though the same source donors were used to generate the Xenium data, the data for each sample originates from a different tissue section and was the result of another protocol / analysis platform besides Visium; therefore the data from the Xenium protocol will have a different Sample Record than the data from the Visium protocol, even for tissue sections from the same source donor.
  • A Series Record is used to link or group together similar samples. For Kwon et al. (2026), two Series Records are used: one series for the results from Visium and one series for the results from Xenium.

GEO curators can package these records into “DataSets” which “represent a curated collection of biologically and statistically comparable GEO Samples”. Researchers can construct queries to search GEO Records and DataSets to find relevant data. The advanced search is especially helpful for this.

Data from GEO can be retrieved using the Bioconductor package GEOquery which is what we will use within the Data Import section of the case study.

The Museum of Spatial Transcriptomics described in Moses and Pachter (2022) is a review meets database, documenting spatial gene expression technologies from 1987 to this decade. While not a comprehensive database of available spatial transcriptomic datasets, it is a comprehensive history of the field and how the technologies have evolved. Rather than using the 2 broad technology categories that we have throughout this case study (e.g., sequencing-based and image-based), which is slightly expanded to 3 in Lim et al. (2025), Moses and Pachter (2022) resource splits the current technologies into 5 categories. It is helpful for better understanding these technologies in greater detail and considering how these technologies have been used in the field (e.g., which technologies are primarily used, from what species are samples analyzed, etc.).

Repository of spatial transcriptomics. CROST encompasses high-quality samples and houses 182 spatial transcriptomic datasets from diverse species, organs, and diseases, comprising 1033 sub-datasets and 48 043 tumor-related spatially variable genes (SVGs). Additionally, it encompasses a standardized spatial transcriptome data processing pipeline, integrates single-cell RNA sequencing deconvolution spatial transcriptomics data, and evaluates correlation, colocalization, intercellular communication, and biological function annotation analyses.

In their paper, the authors say that they developed CROST to facilitate easy access to data, visualizations, and personalized analyses rather than just providing data without the capacity for user-friendly integrated spatial analysis (G. Wang et al. 2024). In this way, CROST doesn’t just offer users access to spatial transcriptomics datasets, it also offers users the ability to explore and better understand those datasets. G. Wang et al. (2024) does mention additional databases where spatial transcriptomics datasets have been collected but notes that these strictly provide data or very limited interactive visualization/analysis in addition to the data.

10X Genomics has created and shared datasets that can be explored and downloaded by researchers. Datasets can be filtered by species, tissue type, preservation method, disease state, and several other parameters. Much of this data may also be available within the GEO.

Using secondary data does require researchers to understand how the data was originally generated and processed to make sure you that they are performing necessary steps and making reasonable comparisons. Combining datasets that have been produced by different groups using different methods could be a challenge and is discussed in Guo et al. (2025). Researchers should also verify that samples can be re-used and were not from donors who only consented to their use within the original study.

An alternative to using secondary data that others have previously generated is to simulate data using tools like scDesign3 (Song et al. 2024). This in silico generation of data can be helpful for testing a workflow or simulating a dataset for some statistical hypothesis testing or other interpretations of real data.

How to work with ST data

There are a variety of tools available to analyze spatial transcriptomics data. Some are command line tools or packages you can load while working with Python or R. Others are more user-friendly tools which point and click interfaces or GUIs. Some of these point and click tools require a license to use while others are free to use. Some of the command line tools / packages are analysis suites while others perform specific tasks such as normalization or downstream analyses. The ITN course “Choosing Genomics Tools” provides a thorough discussion of the available tools. One tool, spatialGE can be used as either an R package (providing functions for a full range of tasks) (Oscar E. Ospina et al. 2022) or as a point and click interface that is free to use (Oscar E. Ospina et al. 2025). In this way, spatialGE is flexible, meeting users where they are at, whether they are comfortable programming or not. Though this case study will focus on working with image-based data for data import, quality control, normalization, and visualization tasks, spatialGE can be used to work with ST data from a variety of technologies and for downstream applications.

Reproducibility considerations

Working with open source tools is an important reproducibility consideration for your research. Use of open source tools promotes transparency and accessibility. Other researchers will have more difficulty reproducing your work if they need to access commercial tools (or a specific version of a commercial tool), than they will if you use open source tools.

Open source tools may be updated over time, so it’s important to combine using open source tools with thorough documentation explaining versions that were used and why choices were made (e.g., parameter values).

If working within a large team, on multiple platforms (e.g., local machine and the cloud or an institutional HPC), or needing to share your computing environment broadly so it can be used by others or at a later time, it may also be worthwhile to create a container with the specific versions of software packages already installed / available. Spatial patterns of expression

Spatially-informed gene expression data is useful for investigating differential expression in multiple contexts: overall transcript abundance, cell-type specific expression, and spatial distribution of expression. The spatial distribution of expression can be visually inspected using quilt plots and more thoroughly investigated using statistical concepts and models. We will discuss some of these downstream uses for exploring spatial patterns of expression.

Differential expression: abundance

When looking at changes in abundance, researchers are comparing gene expression between different time points, different cell types, different cases/conditions (e.g., case and control), etc., specifically looking to see if a gene is upregulated (expressed more) or downregulated (expressed less) in one compared to the other. Statistical models, such as linear or negative binomial, support this type of an analysis when it’s done globally for a whole transcriptome, and the results are visualized using .

Typically Volcano plots are used to display the results of differential gene expression analyses for bulk and single-cell RNAseq. They may also be used for spatial transcriptomics, but are more likely to be used with sequencing-based data than image-based data since image-based methods investigate fewer genes and those genes may have been identified as potentially interesting through previous analyses. For image-based data, other methods will likely be used to show differences in abundance.

The volcano plot is a scatter plot, representing numerical data on both the x- and y-axes. The x-axis of a volcano plot is used to represent the magnitude or amount of change in the expression of a gene. The y-axis is used to represent the statistical significance associated with that change. Because so many genes are being tested, a multiple-hypothesis testing correction should be utilized (e.g., False Discovery Rate or FDR). If a gene shows a magnitude of change greater than a specified cutoff and statistical significance, the points are colored to reflect that they are differentially expressed.

Differential expression: cell type

While volcano plots could be used to visualize the change in expression of genes for a given cell type between different conditions, more likely a violin plot will be used, especially if visualizing the expression of one or only a few genes rather than the whole transcriptome.

Researchers may plot the distribution of the expression of a gene from all cells that have been annotated as a specific cell type in one condition, comparing that to a second distribution of the expression of the same gene from all cells that have been annotated as the same specific cell type in another condition.

Violin plots are not necessarily plotting the results of a statistical test in the same way that volcano plots inherently are. However, you can perform a statistical test, comparing the means of the distributions, and then annotate the violin plot with the result.

Beyond cell-type specific expression, certain biological contexts may also notice a difference in the cell type composition of a tissue. In single-cell analyses, cell types will be annotated using the expression of cell-type marker genes. If a marker gene isn’t observed or is observed far less frequently in a population of cells, excluding this being for a technical reason, this could point to a change in cell composition between conditions.

Differential expression: spatial distribution

Quilt plots are the face of spatial transcriptomics. Quilt plots are a version of a heatmap, displaying data using color. In addition to using color as a primary means to display data, heatmaps generally are two-dimensional. More specifically heatmaps use a grid or matrix with rows and columns, though they are sometimes used to display data with non-grids that are still two-dimensional like maps. However, these are more commonly called choropleths. Plotting the gene expression using color, mapping it to a physical, coordinate-based space shares the same intent as a choropleth.

Quilt plots are primarily used to visualize the expression of a specific gene showing how much it is expressed and where it is expressed. In this case (visualizing continuous numerical data), the legend will be a color gradient bar or color ramp.

However, quilt plots can also be used to plot more discrete or categorical data like cell annotations: spatial domain, cell type, number of expressed genes, discarded vs. kept following quality control.

Beyond the visual inspection of the spatial distribution of a gene, statistical concepts and models support deeper investigation into the spatial distribution of the expression of genes. These are discussed within the Common Next Steps box within the Continued Learning section of the case study.

The human nervous system

The human nervous system allows humans to receive, process, and communicate signals based on input from their surroundings. The nervous system is made up of the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS). The CNS consists of the brain and spinal cord which direct the understanding and communication of received signals to the rest of the nervous system(Coon and Mitterer 2015).

Neurons are a specialized cell type necessary for the communication of these signals within the nervous system and they are cells with an abnormal shape. The body of the cell has the nucleus with branch-like extensions called dendrites which receive signals. Each neuron also has slender projection off of the body of cell called an axon which transmits signals to other cells. The axon has a insulating layer called a myelin sheath to help with signal transduction. Nerves within the nervous system are made up of bundles of axons from multiple neurons(Coon and Mitterer 2015).

The brain has several other specialized cells such as astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. Oligodendrocytes provide the myelin sheath insulation around the axons of neurons.

What is the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex?

The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) is a specific part of the frontal lobe of the cerebral cortex of the brain. The cerebral cortex is the outer layer in the upper part of the brain where cognition is localized. It’s a twisted and folded structure made up of gray matter (spongy tissue made up mostly of the neuronal cell bodies) which sits atop white matter (made up of the neuronal axons and oligodendrocytes). The prefrontal cortex is also known as the “prefrontal area” and is found in the front and top of the frontal lobe. The dorsolateral portion of the prefrontal cortex is towards the top and side of the prefrontal cortex. The dlPFC roughly corresponds with Brodmann area 46 (BA46) and is involved in executive functioning and cognitive control, contributing to working memory and emotional regulation (Kirby et al. 2024).

What is schizophrenia?

Schizophrenia is a chronic neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by a spectrum of symptoms impacting a person’s thoughts, emotions, and behavior Coon and Mitterer (2015). Schizophrenia symptoms are typically first observed in late adolescence (Leucht et al. 2025). Symptoms are generally classified as positive, negative, or cognitive (Noble 2026; Leucht et al. 2025). Positive symptoms refer to mental phenomena that are added experiences beyond a usual experience such as hearing voices or experiencing hallucinations. In contrast, negative symptoms refer to behaviors or emotions that are not present in people with schizophrenia such as withdrawal / social isolation. Cognitive symptoms include attention issues, working and/or verbal memory problems, misunderstandings of social situations, and trouble with critical thinking, adaptation, planning, or problem solving. Cognitive symptoms are related to executive functioning.

The dlPFC has been implicated as an area of interest in schizophrenia research, in part due to the overlap in behaviors associated with that region and with cognitive symptoms, but also due to research. Experimental therapies targeting the dlPFC have been found to improve executive function and working memory (Leucht et al. 2025).

Why collect samples from the dlPFC?

Given that the dlPFC has been implicated in cases of schizophrenia, specifically cognitive symptoms, researchers commonly study this area of the brain for schizophrenia research. Brain imaging studies in vivo (MRI, PET, etc.) do not need to collect samples because such imaging can be done with research participants (Keshavan et al. 2020). To study the molecular biochemistry and omics of this brain region on a larger scale than is possible with live imaging, however, postmortem samples need to be collected.

Why is spatially-informed gene expression data used?

The dlPFC has several laminar domains or layers, each of which seems to have its own structure and molecular signature (e.g., cell composition, gene expression patterns, morphology, connectivity, etc.), and therefore, likely, a unique role in cognitive function. Originally introduced in the early 1900s (Zilles 2018), these layers have been confirmed throughout the years with recent single cell and spatial transcriptomics studies using a data-driven approach in describing them rather than manually annotating them (Maynard et al. 2021; Huuki-Myers et al. 2024).

Given this, spatially-informed gene expression data is beneficial not just for studying how gene expression may differ in abundance between neurotypical cases and cases with diagnosed schizophrenia, and not just for querying if cell types show differences in expression or composition between cases, but also to query if there might be differences among these layers, a point discussed by Maynard et al. (2021). In this way, spatial transcriptomics acts as a hypothesis generation tool either to find candidate patterns that can be assayed further or for initial investigation into mechanisms to interpret previously identified risk genes.

Data generation

Xenium is an image-based spatial transcriptomics platform. Tissue samples are either fresh frozen (FF) or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE). Each of these options has its own specific protocols necessary for further sample preparation. The machine that is used to generate data is called the “Xenium Onboard Analyzer”.

Gene panel

Gene panels may be read-to-use, pre-designed panels, or (usually for more cost) custom RNA panels that focus on a researcher’s specific genes and species of interest.

In the design process, each gene in the panel has a codeword or optical barcode assigned to it that will be the result of probe hybridization and necessary for the eventual decoding of the observed optical signature of what is hybridizing to a transcript into the assignment of gene identity.

Codeword

A distinct sequence or pattern of fluorescent signals, observed across multiple imaging cycles and wavelength channels, that represent a specific target gene. Also known as an “optical barcode”.

The collection of unique / possible codewords for a given gene panel is called the codebook. In the codebook, the optical signatures and the gene they represent are recorded.

Sample preparation

Sectioning and complementary methods

When working with spatial transcriptomics, as previously mentioned, it is common to use complementary spatial transcriptomics methods to explore samples from the same source tissue in order to get a greater depth of information. In these cases, the same sample section cannot be used to generate data from both technologies. Instead, neighboring sections are taken from the tissue block and one section is used with one method while the other section is used with the other method.

Any domain or cell type analysis that is performed on results from one tissue section won’t directly align with directly align with the neighboring tissue section. However, there are algorithms to align coordinates. Kwon et al. (2026) used one of these (spaTransfer) described in the “SpD clustering and annotation” section of the Xenium methods description (Fang et al. 2026).

Xenium Analyzer steps

For a thorough discussion of the Xenium process, consider Lim et al. (2025) or 10X Genomics overview of the Xenium algorithms used by the Xenium Onboard Analyzer.

RNA imaging

Imaging is performed in cycles: Probes which are specific to certain mRNA molecules are applied to the sample and hybridize to the mRNA molecules. Secondary probes with fluorophores are then applied to the sample and imaging occurs and fluorophores are cleaved or extinguished/photobleached before the cycle repeats. Rather than one fluorophore being used to represent one gene, permutations of fluorophores across imaging rounds, (an “optical barcode” or “codeword”) represent a given gene. Therefore, gene-specific fluorescent signals are used to ascertain what genes are expressed, noting their spatial location within the sample. Images are also captured to visualize where DAPI (a proxy for cell nuclei) or cell boundary marker stains are within the sample.

Signal decoding

Following image procurement, RNA molecule signals are identified and filtered to remove background or low quality signal as described by 10X Genomics. The codebook is used to map the observed optical barcode from probes hybridizing to the RNA molecules to the gene it represents.

Nuclear and cell segmentation

Cell segmentation for v1 utilizes DAPI to stain for cell nuclei. After acquiring a DAPI image of the sample, nuclei are segmented to approximate nuclei boundaries. Then an isotropic nuclear expansion algorithm is used to approximate the cell boundaries. An alternative approach (not used for Kwon et al. (2026)) utilizes multiple stains for cell boundaries and segments using those stains rather than expanding the nucleus.

Assignment and quantification

After Signal Decoding and Nuclear and Cell Segmentation have occurred, transcripts can be assigned to cells, Xenium’s quality control can be run, and output files can be generated.

Xenium quality control

Xenium assigns each decoded transcript a Phred-style quality score, signifying a confidence level in that transcript’s identity. Control probes and codewords are necessary for computing these quality scores.

  • Negative control codewords

codewords in the codebook that do not have any probes matching that code. They are chosen to meet the same requirements as regular codewords and can be used to assess the specificity of the decoding algorithm.

This means that a negative control codeword is an optical signature that wouldn’t be expected to be observed in the data because the underlying probes necessary for seeing this specific signal don’t match the probes that were used for any genes. If a negative control codeword is observed, it would be a false positive.

Specificity

Measures a model or process’s ability to correctly identify true negative cases, avoiding false positives. Also known as the True Negative Rate. Specificity or sensitivity (true positive rate) can be prioritized; specificity should be prioritized if false positives are important to avoid.

  • Negative control probes

probes that exist in the panels but target non-biological sequences. They can be used to assess the specificity of the assay.

This means that a negative control probe is a probe that isn’t expected to hybridize to transcripts within the sample. If they hybridize and are visualized, the specificity of the assay is low because.

  • Unassigned codewords

unused codewords. There is no probe in a particular gene panel that will generate the codeword.

This explanation may seem like it overlaps with the “negative control codeword” definition above, and it does seem to in a way. However, this means that an unassigned codeword is an optical signature that wouldn’t be expected to be observed in the data, not by purposeful design (like is the case with negative control codewords) but instead as a byproduct. There are a set number of imaging cycles, a set number of imaging wavelength channels, and a set number of fluorophores. A limited set of permutations of these signals (optical barcodes) are theoretically possible. However, not all of them are assigned to genes in the panel. Some are assigned to negative control codewords, but all of the rest are unassigned codewords.

Xenium’s process for assessing the quality of decoding is explained in this documentation, but highlights are included here:

  • First to find raw quality scores: which codeword best explains the observed fluorescent signals/data for that transcript? What’s the likelihood of observing that codeword compared to others?
  • Bin all raw quality scores and find the proportion of negative control codewords in each bin, using this to calibrate the raw quality scores.
  • Xenium uses the calibrated quality score to filter the cell-feature matrix to include transcripts that only have a quality score above a certain threshold (quality scores greater than or equal to 20). If a transcript is dropped during this process, the count that it would add to the gene isn’t added to the final cell feature matrix.
  • These control codewords and probes are still included in the cell feature matrix that is returned to the researcher.

Secondary analyses (optional)

The Xenium OnBoard Analyzer can be used to perform some secondary or downstream analyses. They are explained in greater detail by Xenium in this documentation. Possibilities include:

  • Filtering (removing any cells with fewer than five transcripts). Note that this filtering is applied prior to creating secondary analysis results, but is not applied to the data outputs returned to users (e.g., the cell feature matrix) outside of secondary analysis results.
  • Dimensionality reduction (PCA, UMAP)
  • Clustering (Graph-based or K-means)
  • Differential Gene Expression

Summary

In summary, Xenium technology utilizes a panel of gene-specific probes to assay the spatially-resolved expression for a targeted set of genes at a single-cell or sub-cellular resolution within tissue samples. Samples for Xenium analysis are prepared from either fresh frozen (FF) or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. While preparation protocols differ by sample type (e.g, FF or FFPE), generally tissue sections are taken from the tissue block, mounted on specialized slides, and undergo chemical treatments for fixation, permeabilization, probe hybridization, and staining. Following sample preparation, imaging cycles are performed using a Xenium Analyzer. The Xenium Analyzer’s onboard software performs nuclear and cellular segmentation to approximate boundaries between cells and assign observed transcripts to individual cells. Xenium platforms use automated real-time quality control to remove noise and lower quality data. Researchers receive a variety of data including data that reports on the localization of cells, nuclei, and transcripts, observed counts for the genes, and morphology images for the tissue sample sections.

In this case study, we will use the tool spatialGE to work with Xenium data from Kwon et al. (2026). Though Kwon et al. (2026) first collected Visium data (that ultimately informed the design of the gene panel), we will only be considering the Xenium data in this case study, especially focusing on the early steps of data processing such as data import, quality control, and normalization as well as the visualization of spatial patterns with Quilt plots.

Spatial coordinates

Classically, -cells.parquet files are used to store the spatial coordinates of the cells, specifically the locations (or centers of the cells).

The .parquet file type is a departure from the normal way we would expect data to be stored. Often our file types (e.g., .csv) store data row by row with each row containing a complete record or set of related information about a specific sample, timepoint, etc. Instead, .parquet files store data column-by-column. This leads to more efficient storage and querying of large data files.

General note on variety of Xenium outputs

Xenium produces a variety of output files. Not all of these will be shared / deposited into an archive. While some file types may be more usual or preferred by some in the field (e.g., because it’s more efficient to store or it’s a file type they are more comfortable with), there doesn’t appear to be a specific industry standard or accepted practice on files to always use. For example, morphology images (likely stained with DAPI to mark nuclei) are often output but are not always shared. An output may be shared as a .csv file instead of a .parquet file or as a Zarr file instead of an .h5 file. It may seem like you can’t proceed if you don’t see the file types you expect, but it’s not necessarily a lost cause. Make sure that you understand:

  1. What files are available and what they represent about the data. You may be able to use an alternate file type for a specific aspect of the data (e.g., spatial coordinates).
  2. What file types your downstream tools expect or can work with.
  3. If you can use a file type that you have in place of a file type that you or a downstream tool usually expects, or at least wrangle it to meet your needs.
  4. What (if any) specialized packages are necessary to read in and work with certain file types.

Limitations

There are some important considerations regarding this data analysis to keep in mind:

Limitations regarding the scope of this case study:

This case study focuses on early steps for working with spatially-informed data produced by a Xenium Analyzer. Though the motivating reference of this case study (Kwon et al. (2026)) generated and processed both Visium and Xenium datasets, this case study only works with a subset of the Xenium data samples. In addition, though Kwon et al. (2026) performed extensive and sophisticated analysis of spatial patterns of expression within the dorsolater prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) tissue samples, this case study primarily focuses on understanding what the data is, how to import it with spatialGE, how spatialGE stores the data, quality control, normalization, and spatial visualizations of expression for specific genes and sample-wide cell type annotations with quilt plots. More downstream analyses are briefly described within a continued learning section.

Limitations regarding the samples and data analysis:

The Xenium algorithm used for cell segmentation for this data, prior to the researcher receiving it, employs a nuclear expansion following DAPI staining. Given that these samples are from brain tissue and the brain has specialized cells with abnormal structure (e.g., neurons with long axon offshoots), the cell segmentation results may not truly reflect cell boundaries in the sample. At the least, this approach is unlikely to be as accurate as the approach that uses cell boundary markers in addition to DAPI staining to segment cell boundaries.

Though care was taken by Kwon et al. (2026) to match clinical and quality demographics for their case vs control samples, demographic matching was not utilized in randomly subselecting the Xenium samples used within this case study.

Atta et al. (2024) and Bhuva et al. (2024) argue caution against the usefulness of a few classical transformation methods, especially for image-based spatially-informed gene expression data. However, we do show the use of those methods within this case study, while introducing the cautions and an employing an alternative suggestion from Atta et al. (2024). Utilizing the tool from Bhuva et al. (2024) is left as a continued learning exercise.

Clustering is not performed directly with the data, rather clustering from Kwon et al. (2026) is brought over to annotate cells with matching libname IDs for each sample. This choice is made for a variety of reasons such as simplifying the case study and to provide learners with an example of bringing in an outside annotation which may be useful for their own work. Given that the filtering approaches are not exactly the same between the original study and this case study, some cells were retained by the case study that were filtered by the original study. In those cases, we borrow cell type assignments from nearby cells, making an assumption that cells with similar types will be located near each other. This impacts a small number of cells in the case study, but could still be a shortcoming.

Ethical Considerations

There are some important ethical considerations when working with data relating to this case study’s main questions.

Post-mortem donors:

Data used within this case study originate from post-mortem human donors. Similar to organ donation, research involving the recently deceased depends upon the generosity or altruism of donors. While not technically human subjects research, there are still several important ethical questions such as those relating to end-of-life decision making and respecting the deceased’s legacy.

As described by (Pentz_etal_2005?) the multidisciplinary expert Consensus Panel on Research with the Recently Dead (CPRRD) crafted ethics guidelines for this type of research, falling into the following categories:

  • Review and oversight
  • Community involvement
  • Eligibility
  • Research procedures
  • Consent
  • Confidentiality
  • Resource use and cost
  • Conclusion of research (e.g., return of remains to family or disclosure of medically relevant research results)

Even with the CPRRD ethics guidelines, ethical questions persist and (Parent_etal_2024?) enumerates these questions in the following way:

  • Common language to describe this research
  • Oversight of proposed research
  • The role of clinical staff
  • Duration of research
  • Infection prevention and control
  • Sources and timing of authorisation
  • Necessary disclosure during authorisation

Schizophrenia:

Schizophrenia is a condition that impacts a substantial proportion of the population (Leucht et al. 2025). To reduce the morbidity and premature mortality associated with schizophrenia, understanding the basis for the condition and developing treatments and therapies are important avenues of research. In particular, there is a genetic component to the development of schizophrenia, and therefore genetic and precision medicine studies are useful (Leucht et al. 2025; Daws_2017?; Fusar-Poli_etal_2022?). Societal stigma is associated with the condition (Dino_etal_2025?; Leucht et al. 2025) and research resources are limited (Daws_2017?), raising ethical questions about how research funds are allocated and used (Daws_2017?), how the condition is treated (Leucht et al. 2025), how individuals with the condition are discussed (Dino_etal_2025?), and how individuals with extreme expressions of symptoms are excluded from research or otherwise expected to pause current treatments in order to participate (Wilson_Stanley_2006?). Leucht et al. (2025) notes that “family psychoeducation is an easily implemented intervention that has been found to improve outcomes for individuals with schizophrenia and potentially reduce stigmatizing attidues within the family.” (Fusar-Poli_etal_2022?) suggests an innovative precision psychiatry roadmap that “links patients, providers, clinical laboratories and researchers through the use of screening, prognostic, diagnostic, and theurapeutic clinical prediction models” in order to “account [for] each person’s variability in genes, environment, and lifestyle”, but notes that it has its own “ethical concerns at the individual, healthcare and whole societal level”.

Another important consideration is using respectiful language to describe those impacted by the condition. According to the National Center on Disability and Journalism Language Style Guide, person-first language (e.g., “a person with schizophrenia” or “a person diagnosed with schizophrenia”) is typically preferred over identity-first descriptions. (Dino_etal_2025?) found that the scientific community has shown improvement in adopting person-first language when discussing this disorder, but that there remains room for improvement.

Data Management:

Researchers need to carefully study secondary data (data collected originally by others) to ensure that they understand how the data were collected and what the variables represent. Use of secondary data, especially participant-level clinical data (e.g., from patients or research participants) requires consideration about how that data was collected and shared, and the consent of participants (Sterling 2011).

Personally Identifiable Information (PII) and Protected Health Information (PHI) need to be managed securely to safeguard patient privacy. Samples collected post-mortem do not strictly fall under the PII or PHI categories, however health information collected before death is still safeguarded. The privacy of the donor needs to be considered and donor information should be managed securely, especially for cases where the condition has stigma associated with it (e.g., for donors previously diagnosed with schizophrenia).

Data that are considered PII or PHI includes names, dates of birth, contact information, account or health plan numbers, IP addresses, and medical records. You can read more about PII and PHI and ethical data handling practices in this course with broader information regarding proactive and ethical data management and sharing can be found in this course.

Research is reproducible when the same methods (code, script, implementation based on description, etc.) can be used on the same data or within the same experimental system by a new researcher to recapitulate the results or findings of the original researcher. Note that just because a research result is reproducible doesn’t mean that it’s correct or valid. Research can be reproducibly wrong. However, reproducibility is always an important step towards useful, valid, and generalizable scientific discovery. However, reproducibility exists on a spectrum, and making a study perfectly reproducible is not an attainable goal. Therefore, taking incremental but tangible steps to make a study more reproducible is important as discussed in this course. Kwon et al. What spatialGE is looking for

spatialGE provides the user flexibility in how they import their data. Users can either provide file paths or they can provide named lists with each list containing data frames for each sample. To better understand the data we have and are working with, we will provide named lists of data frames for both the spatial coordinates and the mRNA/feature counts.

Each named list will use the same sample names to show which data goes with which sample. Each data frame within a given named list will have similar structure, but different underlying data points specific to that sample.

Spatial coordinates

For spatial coordinates, each data frame should have:

  • 3 columns
    • Cell IDs
    • X coordinate for the center of the cell
    • Y coordinate for the center of the cell
  • One row per each unique cell ID; let’s say we have m cells

We will include a column for the area of each cell for later use in the normalization process, but this is not an expected part of the input by spatialGE.

mRNA counts

For the mRNA counts, each data frame should have:

  • n rows
    • 1 for each feature
    • let’s say we have n features.
  • (m+1) columns
    • 1 for each cell
    • For our spatial coordinates, we said we had m cells
    • Add 1 because the first column will list the feature/gene names

Recall that both of these (spatial coordinates and mRNA counts data) will be stored in separate named lists where the names correspond to samples, and each sample has its own data frame.

How does lapply or “loop”ing work?

A function like the getGEOSuppFiles() function from the GEOquery package accepts inputs and performs specified steps with that input, providing some output following those steps.

When you use a loop to call a function for a set of inputs, the loop provides each input individually to the function. For each individual input, the function performs its specified steps and produces an output. Then the loop gives the function the next input and the process is repeated until there are no more inputs to give the function.

We’ll use an example with lab pets to show this in a more concrete way.

We could specify a loop (that doesn’t use or call a function yet), but just goes through each of the lab members who has a cat and outputs their cat’s name. Outside of the loop, we could specify the dog’s name for the fourth lab member who has a dog, not a cat. Because that last part is happening outside of the loop, it is only output once rather than being output every time we go through the loop. And the number of times we go through the loop relates to the number of inputs we’re looping through (in this case 3 because there are 3 lab members with cats).

We could write a function that generalizes that process, specifically reporting the lab member and who their pet is, even specifying whether that pet is a dog or a cat, after evaluating the input. Functions are especially useful in cases like this where there is a process that has related tasks with specifiable, but slightly different behavior dependent on how an input is evaluated.

Notice that we have to explicitly call this function for every lab member that we want to use it for. We would have to call the function four times, one for each lab member. Generally, we want to reduce redundancy in our code, so it might make sense to use a loop to loop through our data and call the function multiple times that way.

To reduce redundancy, we could call the function with a for loop. Notice because of how we defined our function, the lab member with a dog is part of the for loop now, instead of outside of it!

lapply is another function that provides the functionality of a loop, meant specifically to work with functions. One intricacy of lapply is that the output from calling the function for a set of a items is returned in a list. (That’s what the l in lapply stands for: “list”.) Given that we’ve said that we want our spatialGE inputs to be in named lists, lapply will be helpful for us in constructing those inputs down the line.

How to construct an lapply statement

lapply statements have 2 parts.

  1. Some iterable object such as a list or vector. This may be:
  • a named list
  • a vector with character elements
  • a vector of indices (1:length(some_vector)).
  1. Some function to apply for each element of the iterable object. This may be:
  • a base R function
  • a function from a loaded package or library
  • a function you’ve defined elsewhere in your code

If you iterate directly over a named list, the output list from lapply will use the same names from the input for the corresponding output. If you instead iterate using a vector of indices, the list won’t have names and you may need to name the output yourself.

For more explanation and examples of using lapply, consider the post Andrews (2021) or the function’s documentation.

A regex pattern is used to specify which files to download (there are more available than just these 3 for each sample).

Details on the regex pattern

Regex is another term for a regular expression that is used to match a pattern. In this case, we’re matching any one of 3 patterns and asking GEOquery to fetch only the files that match one of these 3 patterns.

  • The “or” like behavior of this is from the vertical pipes (| ) in the regex.
  • The double backslashes (\\) are escape characters which are used in front of special or meta characters (e.g., ., *, -, +) that may otherwise be used for specific, programmatic purposes aside from literal string matching. Using an escape character in front of the special character is necessary to make sure they are considered as literal characters within the string matching rather than any special purpose or role they may serve. If only a single backslash was used, R would evaluate and drop that backslash, passing only the “unescaped” special character to regex. Therefore we have to also escape the backslash that regex uses. R will recognize and drop the first one in its string parsing, while the second backslash will remain and be recognized by the regex parsing, escaping the special characters (a dash or period in this case).

decompress_read_parquet <- function(samples_metadata, sample){
  #Build file paths
  gz_file <- here(paste0("data/raw/",
                         sample,
                         "/",
                         sample,
                         "_",
                         samples_metadata[which(samples_metadata$sampleID == sample), "brainNumID"],
                         "-cells.parquet.gz"))
  
  decompressed_file <- str_replace(gz_file, ".gz", "")

  #Decompress
  R.utils::gunzip(gz_file, destname = decompressed_file, remove = FALSE, skip = TRUE)

  #Read in
  df <- arrow::read_parquet(decompressed_file)

  #Remove the decompressed file because we no longer need it
  file.remove(decompressed_file)
  
  #Select columns of interest
  centroids <- df %>%
    dplyr::select(cell_id, x_centroid, y_centroid, cell_area)

  #reclaim some R resources
  rm(df, gz_file, decompressed_file)
  gc()

  #Return
  return(centroids)

}

  • doesn’t need to decompress them first because read_csv from readr can handle compressed files.
  • uses -cell_boundaries.csv.gz as the file type specific pattern.
  1. We group the data by the cell_id column since multiple rows are present for each cell ID.
  2. (with slice) We have to append the first row for each cell ID also to the bottom in order to “close the polygon” by going back to the starting vertex.
  3. We grab coordinates for each cell’s polygon vertices and store all coordinates in a geometry column.
  4. (with st_as_sf()) We convert the data frame into a simple feature object so that we can work within a spatial context (to compute the cell’s centroid) for non-spatial data structures.
  5. (with st_centroid()) We compute the centroid location for each cell using the geometry column created earlier.
  6. We grab the x and y coordinates for the centroids, storing them in their own columns within the feature object.
  7. (with st_drop_geometry()) We drop the geometry or feature object aspect of the data so that we’ll be able to select just the columns we want in a dataframe.
  8. We store the Cell IDs and centroid coordinates as a dataframe for that sample.

read_rna <- function(samples_metadata, sample){

  h5file <- here(paste0("data/raw/",
                         sample,
                         "/",
                         sample,
                         "_",
                         samples_metadata[which(samples_metadata$sampleID == sample), "brainNumID"],
                        "-cell_feature_matrix.h5"))
                              

  rna_h5data <- rhdf5::h5read(h5file, name = "matrix")

  rna_mat <- Matrix::sparseMatrix(i = rna_h5data$indices,
                       p = rna_h5data$indptr,
                       x = as.integer(rna_h5data$data),
                       dims = rna_h5data$shape,
                       index1 = FALSE) %>%
      as.matrix() %>%
      as.data.frame() %>%
      `rownames<-`(rna_h5data$features$name) %>%
      `colnames<-`(rna_h5data$barcodes) %>%
      tibble::rownames_to_column("genes")

  rm(rna_h5data, h5file)
  gc()

  return(rna_mat)
}

What is this code doing?
  1. We define a function that we can call with lapply in order to process the .h5 files and produce a list to give to spatialGE eventually.
  2. Uses a similar method to read in the data files as our previous decompress_read_parquet() function for the parquet files, but it
  • doesn’t need to decompress them
  • uses -cell_feature_matrix.h5 as the file type specific pattern.
  1. We read in the corresponding cell feature matrix file using h5read() from the rhdf5 package. This package requires a name parameter which corresponds to the name of the top list. In the What are the data? section of the case study, we found that name to be “matrix”.
  2. We build a sparse matrix, using the elements of the .h5 file that correspond to each argument
  • i: specifies the positions or indices of the rows with non-zero values in the matrix – indices from the .h5 file.
  • p: specifies the first column with a non-zero value for each row – indptr from the .h5 file.
  • x: non-zero values for the matrix entries – data from the .h5 file.
  • dims: matrix dimensions – shape from the .h5 file.
  • index1 we set to FALSE because our values are 0-based indices meaning that we start counting indices from 0 instead of 1.
  1. We expand the sparse matrix to be a dense matrix that can then be turned into a data frame.
  2. We set the row names to be the feature names from the .h5 file (features$name)
  3. We set the column names to be the cell IDs from the .h5 file (barcodes)
  4. We also move the rownames to the first column and name it “genes” because spatialGE looks for gene names in the first column, not as rownames, making sure they are unique..
  5. Finally we store this data frame in our named list for that sample

The STlist object

We can look at the type of an object using the typeof() function.

typeof(scz_spatial)
[1] "S4"

The STlist object is an S4 class in R. This means that the object has “slots”. Slots are accessed using an @, rather than a $ (which you would use for a list or data frame column).

Typically you would see the names in a list using the names() function. Instead, for an S4 class object you use the function slotNames().

slotNames(scz_spatial)
[1] "counts"       "spatial_meta" "gene_meta"    "sample_meta"  "tr_counts"   
[6] "gene_krige"   "misc"

With an S4 class, you have to pre-specify slots prior to the object’s creation. You can’t add slots as you go through an analysis. Therefore, some of these slots currently have data and others will have data added once some spatialGE functions are used. In this case, the S4 object spatialGE uses has its slots pre-specified by the package developers. We, the package users, will not be able to create any new slots.

If we tried to assign a new slot, we would see an error.

Using Control Probes and Codewords

First we create a function that we can use with lapply that takes the filtering thresholds that we found previously as well as each counts matrix as inputs. Notice that the thresholds are named arguments meaning they have default values set to the exact variables we were working with earlier. We don’t have to specify them when we call the function because they have these default values. We find the combined expression within each cell of each of the three associated controls using column sums and identify the cell IDs where the combined expression of any one of these types of controls is greater than or equal to the 99th percentile threshold. Finally, we take a union of the identified cell IDs.

By calling this function with lapply, we’ll have a named list (to_remove_all) where each sample name stores a vector of cell IDs that should be removed for that sample.

We use a for loop to then loop through the sample names and actually filter the cell IDs within the count matrix. Recall that the cells correspond to the columns in the count matrix. Our filter is specifically using an exclamation point (!) to specify that if a column name is not the list of cell IDs to be filtered, then keep it.

We use a for loop for the actual filtering because we don’t want to output a list. Rather we want to overwrite the counts slot, doing the same filtering steps for all of the samples (just using a different set of cell IDs to keep).

findOverlap <- function(mat, negcp = negcontrol_probe_cutoff, negcc = negcontrol_cdwd_cutoff, ucc = unassigned_cdwd_cutoff){
    which_negcp <- names(which(mat[startsWith(rownames(mat), "NegControlProbe"), ] %>% Matrix::colSums() >= negcp))
    which_negcc <- names(which(mat[startsWith(rownames(mat), "NegControlCodeword"), ] %>% Matrix::colSums() >= negcc))
    which_ucc <- names(which(mat[startsWith(rownames(mat), "Unassigned"), ] %>% Matrix::colSums() >= ucc))

    to_remove <- union(union(which_negcp, which_negcc), which_ucc)

    return(to_remove)
}

to_remove_all <- lapply(scz_spatial@counts, findOverlap)

for (sample_name in names(to_remove_all)){

}

Normalizing

Transforming the raw mRNA counts is again necessary because of technical biases. Typically if working with single cell RNAseq data or data from sequencing based spatial transcriptomics assays (e.g., Visium), normalization methods try to correct for different sequencing depths in different cells. Typical methods used include log-transformation or the SCTransform method. The spatialGE package allows users to implement either of these with their spatially-resolved gene expression data. However, both of these methods are best suited to working with sequencing-based data rather than image-based data. Atta et al. (2024) and Bhuva et al. (2024) discuss how these traditional methods may not be appropriate for spatial transcriptomics data, especially data from image-based technologies. Atta et al. (2024) recommends using cell volume or cell area when available to normalize image-based data. Bhuva et al. (2024) introduces a method SpaNorm which models library size effects while taking into account the spatial information inherent in the data. The SpaNorm method can’t directly work with an STlist object from spatialGE without first converting it to a SpatialExperiment or Seurat object. Therefore, we won’t perform normalization with SpaNorm within this case study. We will perform normalization with the typical methods and a cell area normalization.

Transformed counts are stored in the tr_counts slot and we can’t store more than one set of transformed counts due to the “slot” nature of the data. Since we want to run more than one method and compare them, we will need multiple different spatialGE STlist objects. We’ll duplicate two here, one that we can use with SCTransform and one we can use with a cell area normalization.

Cell Area Normalization

Atta et al. (2024) recommends using cell volumes to normalize image-based data when it is available, but that cell area can be used instead. We do not have cell volumes, but we do have cell areas.

We recommend, as first choice, cell volume normalization to account for such partial cell capture within imaged regions. Cell area may serve as a proxy where volume estimates are not available.

The spatialGE package does not currently support any additional normalization methods besides log-transformation and SCTransform. However, we can perform an alternate normalization method and store its results in our STlist object (scz_spatial), using data already present in scz_spatial because we brought over the cell_area column for each cell earlier in the Data Import section of the case study.

The cell area normalization procedure requires that:

  • We find the median cell area for each sample
  • Then find cell specific size factors by dividing each cell’s area by the median.
  • We then scale the counts by these size factors.
  • Finally, similarly to the log transformation method, we add a pseudocount of one so that there are no zeros before we use a logarithm to bring the values closer together.

This approach is used in the OSTA book chapter for Xenium workflows and adapts code from the scrapper package.

Beyond performing and storing our cell area normalization, we’ll also want to add the same further modifications to the STlist that spatialGE typically does. You might wonder – ifthose steps are not necessary for the normalization process, why are we doing them? We're going to do them because other functions fromspatialGEmay expect that information to be there. A strength of thespatialGE` package and its web application is that later functions expect quality control and normalization steps were performed. So something that the transformation function does, we’ll want to do so that later steps aren’t expecting it and can’t find it.

run_area_transformation <- function(stlist_obj, sample){
    
    # find median cell area
    median_area <- median(stlist_obj@spatial_meta[[sample]]$cell_area)
    # calculate cell specific size factors based on cell area
    size_factors <- stlist_obj@spatial_meta[[sample]]$cell_area / median_area
    # transform the counts using these size factors
    norm <- t(t(stlist_obj@counts[[sample]])/size_factors)
    # add a pseudocount of one to the transformed values so there are not zeroes and use a logarithm base of 2 
    stlist_obj@tr_counts[[sample]] <- log1p(norm) / log(2)

    # find gene avgs and stdevs and store
    stlist_obj@gene_meta[[sample]] <- tibble::tibble(gene_mean=as.vector(Matrix::rowMeans(stlist_obj@tr_counts[[sample]]))) %>%
      tibble::add_column(gene_stdevs=as.vector(apply(stlist_obj@tr_counts[[sample]], 1, sd))) %>%
      add_column(gene=rownames(stlist_obj@tr_counts[[sample]]), .before=1)

    return(stlist_obj)
}

combine_counts_annot <- function(matannot, matcount, sample_name, gene_name){
    full_join(
        matannot[[sample_name]][,c("libname", "annot")],
        matcount[[sample_name]][gene_name, ] %>% 
            data.frame() %>% 
            rownames_to_column("libname")
    ) %>% `colnames<-`(c("libname", "annot", sample_name))
}

use_combined_data <- function(mat, gene_name, use = "violin", samples = c("Br1139SCZ", "Br5314CTRL")){
  combined_data <- bind_rows(combine_counts_annot(mat@spatial_meta, mat@tr_counts, samples[1], gene_name),
                             combine_counts_annot(mat@spatial_meta, mat@tr_counts, samples[2], gene_name)) %>%
                      pivot_longer(starts_with("Br"),
                                   values_to = "tr_counts",
                                   names_to = "sample") %>%
                      tidyr::drop_na()

  if (use == "violin"){
    return(
      combined_data %>%
        ggplot(aes(x = sample, 
               y = tr_counts, 
               fill = sample)) + 
        geom_violin() + 
        facet_wrap(~annot, scales = "free_y") + 
        theme_bw() + 
        scale_fill_viridis_d(end = 0.6) + 
        ggtitle(gene_name) +
        theme(legend.position = "bottom")
    )
  } else if (use == "summary"){
    table_length <- length(unique(combined_data$annot)) * 2
    return(
      combined_data %>%
        group_by(annot, sample) %>% 
        summarize(
              min_exp = min(tr_counts), 
              max_exp = max(tr_counts), 
              avg_exp = mean(tr_counts),
              median_exp = median(tr_counts),
              var_exp = var(tr_counts)
        ) %>% 
    print(n=table_length)
    )
  } else {
    message("No other downstream uses supported besides 'violin' and 'summary'")
  }
}

Common Next Steps

Several functions provided by spatialGE can be used for downstream analyses further exploring the spatial patterns of expression. Note that many of them require computational resources beyond a personal computer/laptop and are best run with an institutional HPC.

  • Spatial differential expression
  • Spatial enrichment of gene sets
  • Spatial gradients
  • Spatial autocorrelation

The STdiff function from spatialGE further supports the goal of identifying differential expression within the context of spatial distribution.

Description of function from spatialGE documentation

The method tests for differentially expressed genes between groups of spots/cells (e.g., clusters) in a spatial transcriptomics sample. Specifically, the function tests for genes with significantly higher or lower gene expression in one group of spots/cells with respect to the rest of spots/cells in the sample. The method first runs non-spatial linear models on the genes to detect differentially expressed genes. Then spatial linear models with exponential covariance structure are fit on a subset of genes detected as differentially expressed by the non-linear models (sp_topgenes). If running on clusters detected via STclust, the user can specify the assignments using the same parameters (w, k, deepSplit). Otherwise, the assignments are specified by indicating one of the column names in x@spatial_meta. The function uses spaMM::fitme and is computationally expensive even on HPC environments. To run the STdiff using the non-spatial approach (faster), set sp_topgenes=0.

Gene sets are a predefined collection or group of genes that have been grouped together in a set because they share some characteristic such as regulatory mechanisms, functions, or involvement in a pathway. Functions like STEnrich from spatialGE allow users to test whether a given gene set exhibits high expression within a localized area of the tissue.

Description of function from spatialGE documentation

The function performs a randomization test to assess if the sum of distances between cells/spots with high expression of a gene set is lower than the sum of distances among randomly selected cells/spots. The cells/spots are considered as having high gene set expression if the average expression of genes in a set is higher than the average expression plus num_sds times the standard deviation. Control over the size of regions with high expression is provided by setting the minimum number of cells/spots (min_units). This method is a modification of the method devised by Hunter et al. 2021 (zebrafish melanoma study).

Tissue samples can be clustered in order to annotate cell types or domains. Functions like STgradient from spatialGE use linear models and Spearman correlation coefficients to investigate if a gene is expressed more highly in cells closer to a specific reference tissue domain than cells farther away from the reference domain.

Description of function from spatialGE documentation

The STgradient function fits linear models and calculates Spearman coefficients between the expression of a gene and the minimum or average distance of spots or cells to a reference tissue domain. In other words, the STgradient function can be used to investigate if a gene is expressed higher in spots/cells closer to a specific reference tissue domain, compared to spots/cells farther from the reference domain (or vice versa as indicated by the Spearman’s coefficient).

Moran’s I and Geary’s C are global spatial autocorrelation statistics that report how a gene’s expression is correlated with itself spatially. Are areas of high expression located near each other forming clusters or are they dispersed throughout the sample? Rather is there no clear pattern? Cases of positive spatial correlation have a Moran’s I near 1, and, spatially, similar levels of expression are clustered together. Cases of negative spatial correlation have a statistic near -1, and spatially, dissimilar levels of expression are near each other, appearing as if levels of similar expression are dispersed throughout the sample. Cases of spatial randomness do not display dispersion, instead, there’s no clear pattern. Moran’s I for spatial randomness are near 0. Oscar E. Ospina et al. (2022) notes that negative spatial autocorrelation can also be thought of as uniform expression throughout a tissue sample. Geary’s C follows a similar logic but uses a different numerical scale.

The goal of these statistics is to evaluate if the expression of a gene exhibit spatial autocorrelation, in other words, if the expression isn’t spatially random. Therefore, with the use of these statistics comes the question whether the resulting statistic is statistically significantly far from 0. For more information regarding the use of statistical tests and simulations to assess this question, consider this resource on Spatial Autocorrelation.

Description of function from spatialGE documentation

The function computes global spatial autocorrelation statistics (Moran’s I and/or Geary’s C) for the requested genes and samples. Then computation uses the package spdep. The calculated statistics are stored in the STlist, which can be accessed with the get_gene_meta function. For visual comparative analysis, the function compare_SThet can be used afterwards.

Glossary

Term Definition
Barcodes Cell identifiers. Typically IDs of random letters and numbers assigned to cells in single-cell or spatial experiments.
Centroid The point corresponding to the geometric center of a shape. Has an x- and y- coordinate associated with it. Can be found if you know the vertices of a polygon.
Clustering Grouping similar data points together into distinct sets based on shared qualities or data patterns. Specifically, the process of organizing unlabeled data points into groups such that data points in the same group are more similar to each other than to data points in other groups. Some way to compute distance or similarity is required for clustering methods.
Coefficient Represents proportional relationship between variables. Within regression, quantifies how much the dependent variable changes when the independent variable changes by one unit.
Cohort Research participants within the same study.
Color palette Specific combination of colors used together within a visualization to represent data. May be a set of discrete colors from different color families or instead a range of different shades within a color family.
Compressed A way to store data so that it requires less space. Due to the way that it is stored, it's less readable and often needs to be decompressed in order to visually inspect it or work with it.
Coordinates A set of two numbers (x- and y-) used to represent a specific location on the two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate plane, specifically the x-axis communicates how far to move horizontally while the y-value communciates how far to move vertically from the origin of the plane (0,0). This can be visualized by drawing a vertical line from the x-coordinate on x-axis and a horizontal line from the y-coordinate on the y-axis and seeing where they intersect.
Counts The quantitative measurement of RNA molecules detected for specific genes within a spatial location (e.g., spot or cell). May be detected by sequencing or fluorescent imaging within Spatial Transcriptomics experiments. Often represented in a matrix where rows represent counts for a specific gene and columns represent counts for a specific cell.
Deconvolution A computational method used to estimate the types and proportions of individual cells present within a capture location that has a mixture of cells rather than representing a single-cell. Necessary for spatial technologies like Visium with their "spots", but generally can be used for any technology where there a mixture of cells is represented within the capture area.
Differential Gene Expression The difference in expression of a gene between samples or cases. Differential expression is typically thought of as a difference in abundance or amount of expression but may also be a difference in what cell types a gene is expressed in or where a gene is expressed spatially within a tissue.
Directories Folders in a file system.
Distribution A mathematical description of the possible values of a random variable and how often each occurs.
Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex dlPFC. An area of the prefrontal cortex in the frontal lobe of the brain. Situated towards the top and side of the prefrontal cortex. Involved in executive functioning and cognitive control, contributing to working memory and emotional regulation. Found to be central to cognitive dysfunction in schizophrenia.
Doublet A cell segmentation boundary that contains signals or mRNA counts from two or more distinct cells rather than just one cell -- the result of an error in the image segmentation process.
Droplet In single-cell sequencing, droplets are used to physically separate cells prior to sequencing.
Enrichment Overrepresentation of something (e.g., a gene or set of genes) compared to expectation, other sets, or a background set.
Features Used to describe the rows in a gene count matrix. Since genes correspond to the rows, the term features is often used interchangeably with "genes".
Filtering The process of removing low quality or irrelevant data from a biological dataset. Often what will be filtered is identified during quality control.
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded FFPE. A biological sample is acquired through either surgial resection or biopsy and fixed with formalin to arrest cellular processes, crosslink proteins, and "fix" tissues. Then dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration remove water and provide structural support to the sample before embedding the tissue in a paraffin block that can be sectioned or archived for long-term storage without ultra cold freezers. This method preserves tissue structure and morphological details though nucleic acids are often fragmented in the fixation process.
Fresh frozen FF. Fresh frozen samples are biological tissue samples. Typically a sample is acquired through either surgical resection or biopsy and then rapidly frozen (without prior chemical fixation) using liquid nitrogen or dry ice to arrest cellular processes, and stored at ultra low temperatures until sectioning and further sample preparation steps can be performed. This method preserves cellular architecture and the quality nucleic acids for later analysis though overall morphological details may not be preserved. Additionally, any water inside the tissue will turn into ice crystals, distorting structure
GEO Gene Expression Omnibus. A database storing high throughput gene expression datasets deposited by researchers.
GUI Graphical User Interface. May be a web interface or a local application.
Guide Another term for a legend in a plot.
Histology The microscopic structure of a biological tissue, often visualized with a microscope and tissue staining to enhance contrast of structure.
Hypothesis generation The process of formulating new ideas or testable answers for a research question by exploring data and other related knowledge or results within the field.
Image-based technologies Methods that are used to directly visualize and map RNA transcripts using labeled fluorescent probes that bind directly to the transcripts within intact tissue sections. Automated cycles of high-resolution microscopy rather than sequencing methods are used to detect the hybridization of probes to the transcripts. These methods typically offer high cellular resolution but low molecular resolution only being able to be utilized for a limited panel of genes for each sample.
Index To use a location/coordinate, name, or combination of coordinates or names to extract a specific value from a vector, matrix, or data frame.
laminar domains Functional and structural anatomical subdivisions or layers which are distinct, spanning the brain tissue.
lapply A function from base R that allows the user to loop through an input, performing a specified function on each input, returning the results in a list.
libname IDs assigned to "libraries". Used interchangeably with "barcodes" by spatialGE.
log transformation A data transformation typically used for raw counts data because it is heavily right-skewed due to data sparsity. A pseudocount of one is added to raw values and then a logarithm function is applied, resulting in a more bell-shaped normal distribution by pulling far away data closer to the rest of the data.
Loop Automating a process by iterating over a set of inputs and executing the same code for each item in set (or until a stop condition is met).
Matrix A two-dimensional or rectangular structure with horizontal rows and vertical columns that can be used to store related data (like cell-specific gene counts) in entries (where each row and column intersects).
Metadata Descriptive information describing other data. Descriptions may include how or when that data was generated, source organisms, associated patient IDs, or demographic information.
Mitochondrial reads Reads from a sequencing experiment that map to the mitochondrial genome of cells rather than the nuclear genome.
Morphology The physical appearance within a biological sample like cells within a tissue. Histological images contain morphological information. Morphology images can represent the same tissue as a histological image, but they don't use the stains that histology does to visualize the tissue.
mRNA Messenger RNA, a single-stranded nucleic acid which transfers genetic information. Transcribed from DNA, it will be translated into protein.
Multicellular More than one cell is represented.
Neurotypical A term used to describe donors / their brains based on neurological characterisics -- saying they align with the typical expectations for a population. Neurotypical refers to "control" in this case.
Package A bundled collection of code or software designed to perform specific tasks. Users load these packages and consult their documentation in order to use functions for specific tasks.
Panel A specific, curated set of molecular targets such as genes or transcripts.
Parquet A file type that is used to efficiently store large amounts of data because it uses a column-by-column approach.
PCA Principal component analysis. A dimensionality reduction method that finds principal components or orthogonal components that explain variation within the original dataset.
Personally Identifiable Information Data unique to an individual person that can be used to distinguish their identity, including full names, Social Security numbers, contact information, etc.; abbreviated as "PII".
PMI Postmortem Interval. The amount of time between the passing of a donor and when a tissue sample is taken from the donor. This interval of time is typically reported within hours but may be reported in another unit representing measurement of time. Shorten post mortem intervals correlate with higher tissue quality, minimizing the time for natural degradation to occur.
Polygon A multi-sided two-dimensional shape. Each side is a straight line whose endpoints are each connected to an endpoint of another side. Circles or ovals can be approximated by a polygon with many sides.
Postmortem samples Samples collected after the death of the donor.
Protected Health Information A subset of PII that includes any health information that can identify an individual that is regulated by HIPAA, including full name, contact information, medical records, health insurance information, biometric elements, etc.; abbreviated as "PHI".
Pseudobulk A data transformation where counts from single cells are aggregated and considered in bulk, typically by summing counts across all cells of the same cell type within a sample (though counts can be averaged alternatively). Pseudobulks mirror how the data would look if that specific cell population had been isolated and sequenced using a traditional bulk experiment.
Quality Control The process of exploring and evaluating raw biological data to detect technical artifacts, errors, low quality data, biases, etc prior to downstream analysis in order to promote higher confidence results.
Quilt plot A type of plot that is used to visualize the two-dimensional spatial distribution of gene expression or cell types/clusters within a sample, mirroring the physical architecture of the tissue sample. Colors are used to represent the expression values or cell type/domain classes. Can be thought of as a type of heatmap without any clustering.
R-squared Statistical measure relating to the proportion of variability within the dependent varaible that can be explained by the independent variable and the relationship between the variables. Often used as a measure of effect size. Also known as the "coefficient of determination".
Random seed A number provided as an initial starting value for random number generators, promoting the repeatability and reproducibility of random processes.
Repeatability The ability for the same researcher to run the same analysis on the same data and get the same results.
Reproducible A term used to describe research or a process that a new researcher can follow, running the same analysis on the same data and getting the same results as the original researcher..
Resolution Refers to the smallest unit of change or observation scale that can reliably be detected or measured. High resolution means that a scale has fine intervals and small differences can be observed. Low resolution means that a scale has broader intervals and small variations may be missed (e.g., harder to differentiate between individual cells or measure all molecules).
RIN RNA Integrity Number. A measure of sample quality determined by an automated software algorithm. Found by analyzing the electrophoretic separation of RNA molecules according to size, processing the resulting signal traces (electropherograms which representing how much of RNA of different sizes is present in samples) to evaluate and rank the signal features and combinations of features according to their predictive information content. Returns a value representing the overal level of RNA degradation within a sample on a standardized 1-10 scale.
Schizophrenia Chronic neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by a spectrum of symptoms impacting a person's thoughts, emotions, and behavior. Symptoms are generally classified as positive, negative, or cognitive.
Segmentation Process of inferring individual cell boundaries from tissue images, nuclear or other cell surface and interior marker staining, and transcript coordinates. Some algorithms if only provided nuclear staining first segment nuclear boundaries and expand them to approximate the cell boundaries. Others use the combination of stains to first find cell boundaries. After inferring cell boundaries, observed transcripts can be assigned to cells.
Sequencing A laboratory technique that extracts and analyzes the genetic material from a sample.
Sequencing-based technologies Methods that utilize microscopic regions or spots on specialized slides to sequence intact tissues, retaining the original physical location of observed RNA molecules within a tissue sample. These methods have high molecular resolution but low cellular resolution because they sequence the whole transcriptome rather than targeting a limited set of genes. However, the spots are not necessarily single-cells and data must be deconvoluted.
Slope Coefficient within a linear model that represents both the steepness and direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variable.
Sparse Term used to describe the results from a single-cell or spatial transcriptomics experiment. Typically means that an experiment is low coverage, possibly because a given cell has a limited number of molecules that are being expressed at any given time. Within a data matrix, this looks like a lot of zeros. A sparse-matrix representation, on the other hand, omits these zeros, storing only the location of and values of non-zero data (though displays of this data may include periods instead of zeros if inspected). This sparse representation is used to reduce the overall storage size needed for the data.
Spatial Transcriptomics Spatial Transcriptomics refers to molecular methods used to measure gene expression at or near single-cell resolution, reporting where observed transcripts are physically located within the source tissue, preserving spatial context.
Specialized cell A highly differentiated cell that serves a specific purpose within a tissue. It may have a unique structure (physical shape or size) compared to other cells. It will express specific genes that other cells may not.
Spot Refers to the regions across a tissue section that sequencing-based methods use to capture spatially-informed data. Spots often represent 10s to 100s of cells (e.g., multicellular) rather than single-cells. Each spot has a defined set of x- and y- coordinates on the slide used to capture data, relating captured data to physical locations within the tissue.
STlist An S4 object that the spatialGE program uses to store spatially-informed expression data for one or multiple samples. Utilizes slots for various aspects of the data and named lists within each slot to store data specific to each sample.
Subcellular Represents part of a cell rather than a whole, single cell.
Transcriptome Complete collection of transcripts produced by the genome in a specific cell, tissue, or organism.
Vertex Where the endpoints of two edges meet in a polygon.
Violin plot A plot used to visualize distributions of numerical variables. Because they visualize the density of data across possible values of the variable, they may resemble violins. Useful for comparing the variable across different biological groups.
Visium A popular sequencing-based technology for generating spatially-informed expression data.
Volcano plot A scatter plot that plots data points according to the observed magnitude of change on the x-axis and the statistical significance on the y-axis. Typically one point represents one gene, and the values are found by comparing the expression of that gene across different experimental conditions or clinical cases. Points are often colored differently based on whether the gene is upregulated (over-expressed) or downregulated (under-expressed) in a specific case relative to a conrol and whether the observed change reached pre-defined thresholds for statistical significance.
Xenium A popular image-based technology for geenrating spatially-informed expression data.
