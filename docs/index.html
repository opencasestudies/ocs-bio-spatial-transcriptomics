Motivation

This case study explores working with spatially-resolved gene expression data, also known as , to explore .

Differential Gene Expression

The difference in expression of a gene between samples or cases. Differential expression is typically thought of as a difference in abundance or amount of expression, but may also be a difference in what cell types a gene is expressed in or where a gene is expressed spatially within a tissue.

Spatial Transcriptomic methods produce data that allow for gene expression to be investigated and described within a spatial context.

Spatial Transcriptomics

Spatial Transcriptomics refers to molecular methods used to measure gene expression at or near single-cell resolution, reporting where observed transcripts are physically located within the source tissue, preserving spatial context.

In this way, researchers can explore where a gene is expressed within a tissue and how that may change over time, based on experimental conditions, or for different clincal contexts. Spatial transcriptomics is a rapidly-evolving area of development that can be used for for a variety of biological questions such as describing cell neighborhoods, cell-cell interactions, and discovering biomarkers or drug targets.

The types of technology used to generate spatial transcriptomics data are generally categorized as or and lie across two inverse spectra: Molecular and Cellular Resolution. Sequencing-based technologies typically provide a whole view (broad molecular resolution), but not at a single-cell or sub-cellular resolution – instead at a Region of Interest (ROI) or resolution (where a “spot” is made up of tens to hundreds of cells.) In other words, sequencing-based technologies provide low precision / cellular resolution. In contrast, image-based technologies typically provide data for a panel-based subset or a limited selection of the transcriptome (in other words, narrow molecular resolution). However, image-based technologies provide data at a single-cell or even sub-cellular resolution (high precision cellular resolution). Due to this, researchers may first collect spatially-resolved gene expression data with a sequencing-based technology for initial hypothesis generation to identify a specific region of interest in a tissue or specific genes to probe where greater cellular resolution would be beneficial, and then follow-up with generating a higher cellular resolution spatially-resolved dataset using an image-based technology. Kwon et al. (2026) is a study posted to bioRxiv recently where the researchers did just this: first using a sequencing-based technology followed by an image-based technology as complementary spatial transcriptomic approaches to study the impacts of within the , microenvironments, and cell types of from the human .

There are several tools available to analyze spatial transcriptomics data. Some are command line tools or packages you can load while working with Python or R. Others are more user-friendly tools which point and click interfaces or GUIs. Some of these point and click tools require a license to use while others are free to use. Some of the command line tools / packages are analysis suites while others perform specific tasks such as normalization or downstream analyses. The ITN course “Choosing Genomics Tools” provides a thorough discussion of the available tools. One tool, spatialGE can be used as either an R package (providing functions for a full range of tasks) (Oscar E. Ospina et al. 2022) or as a point and click interface that is free to use (Oscar E. Ospina et al. 2025). In this way, spatialGE is flexible, meeting users where they are at whether they are comfortable programming or not. Though this case study will focus on working with Xenium data for data import, quality control, normalization, and visualization tasks, spatialGE can be used to work with ST data from a variety of technologies and for downstream applications.

Summary

In summary, Xenium technology utilizes a panel of gene-specific probes to assay the spatially-resolved expression for a targeted set of genes at a single-cell or sub-cellular resolution within tissue samples. Samples for Xenium analysis are prepared from either fresh frozen (FF) or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. While preparation protocols differ by sample type (e.g, FF or FFPE), generally tissue sections are taken from the tissue block, mounted on specialized slides, and undergo chemical treatments for fixation, permeabilization, probe hybridization, and staining. Following sample preparation, imaging cycles are performed using a Xenium Analyzer. The Xenium Analyzer’s onboard software performs nuclear and cellular segmentation to approximate boundaries between cells and assign observed transcripts to individual cells. Xenium platforms use automated real-time quality control to remove noise and lower quality data. Researchers receive a variety of data including data that reports on the localization of cells, nuclei, and transcripts, observed counts for the transcripts, and morphology images for the tissue sample sections.

What are the data?

Data context

The data used in this case study are from Kwon et al. (2026) which collected dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) samples postmortem from neurotypical donors and donors with diagnosed schizophrenia. Samples were fresh frozen following collection prior to spatial transcriptomics dataset generation. Kwon et al. (2026) generated data using both the Visium and Xenium platforms, using analysis of the Visium data to inform what Xenium data to collect. Visium data was generated for all donors as explained in Supplementary Table 1 while Xenium data was generated for a subset of these donors. For donors where both Visium and Xenium datasets were generated, the tissue samples originated from the same fresh frozen tissue block or source for each donor, but the samples themselves (used to generate the data for each platform) are from adjacent tissue sections of each block. Since each platform requires its own slide mounting and chemical reagents, typically serial tissue sectioning is used to yield adjacent / comparable tissue samples that can be used for multiple experiments with different technologies.

Postmortem tissue samples from 24 donors were used to generate Xenium data. Twelve of those donors had been diagnosed with schizophrenia (denoted by SCZ within the study and this case study) while the other 12 were neurotypical (denoted by NTC within the study and CTRL within this case study).

This case study will work solely with the Xenium data, using only 6 of the 24 samples (3 CTRL and 3 SCZ).

We will work with a reduced number of samples in order to keep the size of the data more manageable. It’s important, especially in studies involving visualization of tissue sections, to work with multiple tissue sections per case (e.g., CTRL vs SCZ) to make sure that signal is not an artifact of how that sample was processed or cut and instead is generalizable across tissue sections/samples.

Matching sample demographics

Typically studies match donor demographics between case cohorts (e.g., having comparable average ages or number of individuals of each sex). Kwon et al. (2026) does this for all of the samples overall as well as the subset of samples that were processed with the Xenium platform, reporting the information in Supplementary Table 1.

The Xenium spatial transcriptomics data represents cellular mRNA counts for panel-targeted genes, mapping the transcripts from those genes to the cellular spatial locations in the tissue (where the probes bound, signifying expression). Spatial coordinates

Classically, -cells.parquet files are used to store the spatial coordinates of the cells, specifically the locations (or centers of the cells).

The .parquet file type is a departure from the normal way we would expect data to be stored. Often our file types (e.g., .csv) store data row by row with each row containing a complete record or set of related information about a specific sample, timepoint, etc. Instead, .parquet files store data column-by-column. This leads to more efficient storage and querying of large data files.

Additional information provided in this file is described by 10X genomics.

If -cells.parquet files are not available

Sometimes when you are using publicly available data rather than generating new datasets yourself, files that you may expect are not deposited or archived.

If .parquet files are not available, first check if cells.csv.gz is available as this is an alternate format of the typical file. If not, you can use a different Xenium output to calculate the centroids typically stored in the cells.parquet files. This will be explored further within a click to expand section in the Data Import section of the case study, but this alternate file type is also introduced here:

-cell_boundaries.csv.gz also stores spatial coordinates. Specifically these files have many rows per cell describing each vertex that is part of a polygon, where the polygon edges represent the borders of the cell. We can find the centroid of each cell by considering all of these polygon coordinates together. The contents of this file are the result of the nucleus segmentation algorithm that expands the nuclei DAPI-stained boundaries to determine cell boundaries.

Variable Details
cell_id A cell ID corresponding to the barcode variable in the mRNA counts data. Multiple rows will have the same cell_id value in these files.
vertex_x The x-coordinate of a vertex between two of the edges of the polygon representing that cell.
vertex_y The corresponding y-coordinate of that vertex between two of the edges of the polygon representing that cell.

Another file output that Xenium provides is nucleus_boundaries. This file is not sufficient for cell spatial coordinates because it’s only providing the boundaries of the nucleus rather than the cells. Nuclear positioning varies across cells, so you can’t make an assumption about the center of cell if you have coordinates for the nucleus.

These file types are described in greater detail by 10X genomics.

This case study will use the arrow package to work with -cells.parquet files for the spatial coordinates data.

mRNA counts

The data for the mRNA counts that we’ll be using is stored in -cell_feature_matrix.h5 files. This file type efficiently stores large amounts of data that are typically represented by large matrices (n rows for genes * m columns for cells with counts for a given gene in a specific cell stored at each intersection). Instead, .h5 files use nested named lists to store the data. Of important note, only non-zero counts are stored as well as the indices where those counts would be located in a matrix (e.g., indices for which genes and cell IDs they would correspond with). These nested named lists can be used to reconstruct the matrix structure of the data.

Note that “features” is an alternative word for genes in this field and will be used interchangeably throughout this case study.

General note on variety of Xenium outputs

Xenium produces a variety of output files. Not all of these will be shared / deposited into an archive. While some file types may be more usual or preferred by some in the field (e.g., because it’s more efficient to store or it’s a file type they are more comfortable with), there doesn’t appear to be a specific industry standard or accepted practice on files to always use. For example, morphology images (likely stained with DAPI to mark nuclei) are often output but are not always shared. An output may be shared as a .csv file instead of a .parquet file or as a Zarr file instead of an .h5 file. How does lapply or “loop”ing work?

A function like the getGEOSuppFiles() function from the GEOquery package accepts inputs and performs specified steps with that input, providing some output following those steps.

When you use a loop to call a function for a set of inputs, the loop provides each input individually to the function. For each individual input, the function performs its specified steps and produces an output. Then the loop gives the function the next input and the process is repeated until there are no more inputs to give the function.

We’ll use an example with lab pets to show this in a more concrete way.

We could specify a loop (that doesn’t use or call a function yet), but just goes through each of the lab members who has a cat and outputs their cat’s name. Outside of the loop, we could specify the dog’s name for the fourth lab member who has a dog, not a cat. Because that last part is happening outside of the loop, it is only output once rather than being output every time we go through the loop. And the number of times we go through the loop relates to the number of inputs we’re looping through (in this case 3 because there are 3 lab members with cats).

We could write a function that generalizes that process, specifically reporting the lab member and who their pet is, even specifying whether that pet is a dog or a cat, after evaluating the input. Functions are especially useful in cases like this where there is a process that has related tasks with specifiable, but slightly different behavior dependent on how an input is evaluated.

Notice that we have to explicitly call this function for every lab member that we want to use it for. We would have to call the function four times, one for each lab member. Generally, we want to reduce redundancy in our code, so it might make sense to use a loop to loop through our data and call the function multiple times that way.

To reduce redundancy, we could call the function with a for loop. Notice because of how we defined our function, the lab member with a dog is part of the for loop now, instead of outside of it!

lapply is another function that provides the functionality of a loop, meant specifically to work with functions. One intricacy of lapply is that the output from calling the function for a set of a items is returned in a list. (That’s what the l in lapply stands for: “list”.) Given that we’ve said that we want our spatialGE inputs to be in named lists, lapply will be helpful for us in constructing those inputs down the line.

How to construct an lapply statement

lapply statements have 2 parts.

  1. Some iterable object such as a list or vector. This may be:
  • a named list
  • a vector with character elements
  • a vector of indices (1:length(some_vector)).
  1. Some function to apply for each element of the iterable object. This may be:
  • a base R function
  • a function from a loaded package or library
  • a function you’ve defined elsewhere in your code

If you iterate directly over a named list, the output list from lapply will use the same names from the input for the corresponding output. If you instead iterate using a vector of indices, the list won’t have names and you may need to name the output yourself.

For more explanation and examples of using lapply, consider the post Andrews (2021) or the function’s documentation.

A regex pattern is used to specify which files to download (there are more available than just these 3 for each sample).

Details on the regex pattern

Regex is another term for a regular expression that is used to match a pattern. In this case, we’re matching any one of 3 patterns and asking GEOquery to fetch only the files that match one of these 3 patterns.

  • The “or” like behavior of this is from the vertical pipes (| ) in the regex.
  • The double backslashes (\\) are escape characters which are used in front of special or meta characters (e.g., ., *, -, +) that may otherwise be used for specific, programmatic purposes aside from literal string matching. Using an escape character in front of the special character is necessary to make sure they are considered as literal characters within the string matching rather than any special purpose or role they may serve. If only a single backslash was used, R would evaluate and drop that backslash, passing only the “unescaped” special character to regex. Therefore we have to also escape the backslash that regex uses. R will recognize and drop the first one in its string parsing, while the second backslash will remain and be recognized by the regex parsing, escaping the special characters (a dash or period in this case).

decompress_read_parquet <- function(samples_metadata, sample){
  #Build file paths
  gz_file <- here(paste0("data/raw/",
                         sample,
                         "/",
                         sample,
                         "_",
                         samples_metadata[which(samples_metadata$sampleID == sample), "brainNumID"],
                         "-cells.parquet.gz"))
  
  decompressed_file <- str_replace(gz_file, ".gz", "")

  #Decompress
  R.utils::gunzip(gz_file, destname = decompressed_file, remove = FALSE, skip = TRUE)

  #Read in
  df <- arrow::read_parquet(decompressed_file)

  #Remove the decompressed file because we no longer need it
  file.remove(decompressed_file)
  
  #Select columns of interest
  centroids <- df %>%
    dplyr::select(cell_id, x_centroid, y_centroid)

  #reclaim some R resources
  rm(df, gz_file, decompressed_file)
  gc()

  #Return
  return(centroids)

}

read_rna <- function(samples_metadata, sample){

  h5file <- here(paste0("data/raw/",
                         sample,
                         "/",
                         sample,
                         "_",
                         samples_metadata[which(samples_metadata$sampleID == sample), "brainNumID"],
                        "-cell_feature_matrix.h5"))
                              

  rna_h5data <- rhdf5::h5read(h5file, name = "matrix")

  rna_mat <- Matrix::sparseMatrix(i = rna_h5data$indices,
                       p = rna_h5data$indptr,
                       x = as.integer(rna_h5data$data),
                       dims = rna_h5data$shape,
                       index1 = FALSE) %>%
      as.matrix() %>%
      as.data.frame() %>%
      `rownames<-`(rna_h5data$features$name) %>%
      `colnames<-`(rna_h5data$barcodes) %>%
      rownames_to_column("genes")

  rm(rna_h5data, h5file)
  gc()

  return(rna_mat)
}

The STlist object

We can look at the type of an object using the typeof() function.

typeof(scz_spatial)
[1] "S4"

The STlist object is an S4 class in R. This means that the object has “slots”. Slots are accessed using an @, rather than a $ (which you would use for a list or data frame column).

Typically you would see the names in a list using the names() function. Instead, for an S4 class object you use the function slotNames().

slotNames(scz_spatial)
[1] "counts"       "spatial_meta" "gene_meta"    "sample_meta"  "tr_counts"   
[6] "gene_krige"   "misc"

With an S4 class, you have to pre-specify slots prior to the object’s creation. You can’t add slots as you go through an analysis. Therefore, some of these slots currently have data and others will have data added once some spatialGE functions are used. In this case, the S4 object spatialGE uses has its slots pre-specified by the package developers. We, the package users, will not be able to create any new slots.

If we tried to assign a new slot, we would see an error.

scz_spatial@test <- "This is a test"

Error in (function (cl, name, valueClass) : ‘test’ is not a slot in class “STlist”

Filtering

Using Control Probes and Codewords

First we create a function that we can use with lapply that takes the filtering thresholds that we found previously as well as each counts matrix as inputs. Notice that the thresholds are named arguments meaning they have default values set to the exact variables we were working with earlier. We don’t have to specify them when we call the function because they have these default values. We find the combined expression within each cell of each of the three associated controls using column sums and identify the cell IDs where the combined expression of any one of these types of controls is greater than or equal to the 99th percentile threshold. Finally, we take a union of the identified cell IDs.

By calling this function with lapply, we’ll have a named list (to_remove_all) where each sample name stores a vector of cell IDs that should be removed for that sample.

We use a for loop to then loop through the sample names and actually filter the cell IDs within the count matrix. Recall that the cells correspond to the columns in the count matrix. Our filter is specifically using an exclamation point (!) to specify that if a column name is not the list of cell IDs to be filtered, then keep it.

We use a for loop for the actual filtering because we don’t want to output a list. Rather we want to overwite the counts slot, doing the same filtering steps for all of the samples (just using a different set of cell IDs to keep).

findOverlap <- function(mat, negcp = negcontrol_probe_cutoff, negcc = negcontrol_cdwd_cutoff, ucc = unassigned_cdwd_cutoff){
    which_negcp <- names(which(mat[startsWith(rownames(mat), "NegControlProbe"), ] %>% Matrix::colSums() >= negcp))
    which_negcc <- names(which(mat[startsWith(rownames(mat), "NegControlCodeword"), ] %>% Matrix::colSums() >= negcc))
    which_ucc <- names(which(mat[startsWith(rownames(mat), "Unassigned"), ] %>% Matrix::colSums() >= ucc))

    to_remove <- union(union(which_negcp, which_negcc), which_ucc)

    return(to_remove)
}

to_remove_all <- lapply(scz_spatial@counts, findOverlap)

for (sample_name in names(to_remove_all)){
    scz_spatial@counts[[sample_name]] <- scz_spatial@counts[[sample_name]][,!colnames(scz_spatial@counts[[sample_name]]) %in% to_remove_all[[sample_name]]]
}

The library size is the total sum of the counts in a single cell across genes.

The number of features in a cell is the count of unique genes that have a count of at least one in a given cell.

Both the library size and the number of features in a cell serve as indicators of cell quality. Cells may be damaged during the sample extraction or preparation steps

Normalizing

Transforming the raw mRNA counts is again necessary because of technical biases. Typically if working with single cell RNAseq data or data from sequencing based spatial transcriptomics assays (e.g., Visium), normalization methods try to correct for different sequencing depths in different cells.

Transformation with spatialGE can be performed with either the log-transformation or the SCTransform method. Both of these methods are best suited to working with

https://bioconductor.org/books/release/OSTA/pages/ind-normalization.html

In the future, would like to use an alternative normalization method like using cell area or SpaNorm and adding result to the tr_counts slot instead of using SCTransform since this data is imaging based instead of sequencing based. We can get cell area from the parquet files. We would have to join that information with the @spatial_meta slot.

We can only perform one transformation of the data since the transformed counts will be stored in the tr_counts slot and we can’t create another one. If we wanted to run both and compare them, we would need two different spatialGE STlist objects.

We’re going to make quilt plots showing the expression of these genes for each spot/cell in two samples (one NTC and one SCZ).

Note that quilt plots don’t require histology image (image of the tissue), because the spatial coordinates data that we input provides x and y coordinates for the center of each cell/spot. Therefore, we can place the location of these cells without a tissue image.

Also note that this is Xenium data which means there are a lot of cells! So we’re going to use the ptsize argument to set the point size of the spots or cells to be really small. If we make it too large, it’s dense/cramped/hard to view the quilt plot

combine_counts_annot <- function(matannot, matcount, sample_name, gene_name){
    full_join(
        matannot[[sample_name]][,c("libname", "annot")],
        matcount[[sample_name]][gene_name, ] %>% 
            data.frame() %>% 
            rownames_to_column("libname")
    ) %>% `colnames<-`(c("libname", "annot", sample_name))
}

use_combined_data <- function(mat, gene_name, use = "violin", samples = c("Br1139SCZ", "Br5314CTRL")){
  combined_data <- bind_rows(combine_counts_annot(mat@spatial_meta, mat@tr_counts, samples[1], gene_name),
                             combine_counts_annot(mat@spatial_meta, mat@tr_counts, samples[2], gene_name)) %>%
                      pivot_longer(starts_with("Br"),
                                   values_to = "tr_counts",
                                   names_to = "sample") %>%
                      drop_na()

  if (use == "violin"){
    return(
      combined_data %>%
        ggplot(aes(x = sample, 
               y = tr_counts, 
               fill = sample)) + 
        geom_violin() + 
        facet_wrap(~annot, scales = "free_y") + 
        theme_bw() + 
        scale_fill_viridis_d(end = 0.6) + 
        ggtitle(gene_name) +
        theme(legend.position = "bottom")
    )
  } else if (use == "summary"){
    table_length <- length(unique(combined_data$annot)) * 2
    return(
      combined_data %>%
        group_by(annot, sample) %>% 
        summarize(
              min_exp = min(tr_counts), 
              max_exp = max(tr_counts), 
              avg_exp = mean(tr_counts),
              median_exp = median(tr_counts),
              var_exp = var(tr_counts)
        ) %>% 
    print(n=table_length)
    )
  } else {
    message("No other downstream uses supported besides 'violin' and 'summary'")
  }
}

Centroid The point corresponding to the geometric center of a shape. Has an x- and y- coordinate associated with it. Can be found if you know the vertices of a polygon.
Clustering Grouping similar data points together into distinct sets based on shared qualities or data patterns. Specifically, the process of organizing unlabeled data points into groups such that data points in the same group are more similar to each other than to data points in other groups. Some way to compute distance or similarity is required for clustering methods.
Coefficient Represents proportional relationship between variables. Within regression, quantifies how much the dependent variable changes when the independent variable changes by one unit.
Cohort Research participants within the same study.
Color palette Specific combination of colors used together within a visualization to represent data. May be a set of discrete colors from different color families or instead a range of different shades within a color family.
Compressed A way to store data so that it requires less space. Due to the way that it is stored, it's less readable and often needs to be decompressed in order to visually inspect it or work with it.
Coordinates A set of two numbers (x- and y-) used to represent a specific location on the two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate plane, specifically the x-axis communicates how far to move horizontally while the y-value communciates how far to move vertically from the origin of the plane (0,0). This can be visualized by drawing a vertical line from the x-coordinate on x-axis and a horizontal line from the y-coordinate on the y-axis and seeing where they intersect.
Counts The quantitative measurement of RNA molecules detected for specific genes within a spatial location (e.g., spot or cell). May be detected by sequencing or fluorescent imaging within Spatial Transcriptomics experiments. Often represented in a matrix where rows represent counts for a specific gene and columns represent counts for a specific cell.
Deconvolution A computational method used to estimate the types and proportions of individual cells present within a capture location that has a mixture of cells rather than representing a single-cell. Necessary for spatial technologies like Visium with their "spots", but generally can be used for any technology where there a mixture of cells is represented within the capture area.
Differential Gene Expression The difference in expression of a gene between samples or cases. Differential expression is typically thought of as a difference in abundance or amount of expression but may also be a difference in what cell types a gene is expressed in or where a gene is expressed spatially within a tissue.
Directories Folders in a file system.
Distribution A mathematical description of the possible values of a random variable and how often each occurs.
Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex dlPFC. An area of the prefrontal cortex in the frontal lobe of the brain. Situated towards the top and side of the prefrontal cortex. Involved in executive functioning and cognitive control, contributing to working memory, divergent thinking, executive attention, and decision making. Found to be central to cognitive dysfunction in schizophrenia.
Doublet A cell segmentation boundary that contains signals or mRNA counts from two or more distinct cells rather than just one cell -- the result of an error in the image segmentation process.
Droplet In single-cell sequencing, droplets are used to physically separate cells prior to sequencing.
Enrichment Overrepresentation of something (e.g., a gene or set of genes) compared to expectation, other sets, or a background set.
Features Used to describe the rows in a gene count matrix. Since genes correspond to the rows, the term features is often used interchangeably with "genes".
Filtering The process of removing low quality or irrelevant data from a biological dataset. Often what will be filtered is identified during quality control.
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded FFPE. A biological sample is acquired through either surgial resection or biopsy and fixed with formalin to arrest cellular processes, crosslink proteins, and "fix" tissues. Then dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration remove water and provide structural support to the sample before embedding the tissue in a paraffin block that can be sectioned or archived for long-term storage without ultra cold freezers. This method preserves tissue structure and morphological details though nucleic acids are often fragmented in the fixation process.
Fresh frozen FF. Fresh frozen samples are biological tissue samples. Typically a sample is acquired through either surgical resection or biopsy and then rapidly frozen (without prior chemical fixation) using liquid nitrogen or dry ice to arrest cellular processes, and stored at ultra low temperatures until sectioning and further sample preparation steps can be performed. This method preserves cellular architecture and the quality nucleic acids for later analysis though overall morphological details may not be preserved. Additionally, any water inside the tissue will turn into ice crystals, distorting structure
GEO Gene Expression Omnibus. A database storing high throughput gene expression datasets deposited by researchers.
GUI Graphical User Interface. May be a web interface or a local application.
Guide Another term for a legend in a plot.
Histology The microscopic structure of a biological tissue, often visualized with a microscope and tissue staining to enhance contrast of structure.
Hypothesis generation The process of formulating new ideas or testable answers for a research question by exploring data and other related knowledge or results within the field.
Image-based technologies Methods that are used to directly visualize and map RNA transcripts using labeled fluorescent probes that bind directly to the transcripts within intact tissue sections. Automated cycles of high-resolution microscopy rather than sequencing methods are used to detect the hybridization of probes to the transcripts. These methods typically offer high cellular resolution but low molecular resolution only being able to be utilized for a limited panel of genes for each sample.
Index To use a location/coordinate, name, or combination of coordinates or names to extract a specific value from a vector, matrix, or data frame.
laminar domains Functional and structural anatomical subdivisions or layers which are distinct, spanning the brain tissue.
lapply A function from base R that allows the user to loop through an input, performing a specified function on each input, returning the results in a list.
libname IDs assigned to "libraries". Used interchangeably with "barcodes" by spatialGE.
log transformation A data transformation typically used for raw counts data because it is heavily right-skewed due to data sparsity. A pseudocount of one is added to raw values and then a logarithm function is applied, resulting in a more bell-shaped normal distribution by pulling far away data closer to the rest of the data.
Loop Automating a process by iterating over a set of inputs and executing the same code for each item in set (or until a stop condition is met).
Matrix A two-dimensional or rectangular structure with horizontal rows and vertical columns that can be used to store related data (like cell-specific gene counts) in entries (where each row and column intersects).
Metadata Descriptive information describing other data. Descriptions may include how or when that data was generated, source organisms, associated patient IDs, or demographic information.
Mitochondrial reads Reads from a sequencing experiment that map to the mitochondrial genome of cells rather than the nuclear genome.
Morphology The physical appearance within a biological sample like cells within a tissue. Histological images contain morphological information. Morphology images can represent the same tissue as a histological image, but they don't use the stains that histology does to visualize the tissue.
mRNA Messenger RNA, a single-stranded nucleic acid which transfers genetic information. Transcribed from DNA, it will be translated into protein.
Multicellular More than one cell is represented.
Neurotypical A term used to describe donors / their brains based on neurological characterisics -- saying they align with the typical expectations for a population. Neurotypical refers to "control" in this case.
Package A bundled collection of code or software designed to perform specific tasks. Users load these packages and consult their documentation in order to use functions for specific tasks.
Panel A specific, curated set of molecular targets such as genes or transcripts.
Parquet A file type that is used to efficiently store large amounts of data because it uses a column-by-column approach.
PCA Principal component analysis. A dimensionality reduction method that finds principal components or orthogonal components that explain variation within the original dataset.
Personally Identifiable Information Data unique to an individual person that can be used to distinguish their identity, including full names, Social Security numbers, contact information, etc.; abbreviated as "PII".
PMI Postmortem Interval. The amount of time between the passing of a donor and when a tissue sample is taken from the donor. This interval of time is typically reported within hours but may be reported in another unit representing measurement of time. Shorten post mortem intervals correlate with higher tissue quality, minimizing the time for natural degradation to occur.
Polygon A multi-sided two-dimensional shape. Each side is a straight line whose endpoints are each connected to an endpoint of another side. Circles or ovals can be approximated by a polygon with many sides.
Postmortem samples Samples collected after the death of the donor.
Protected Health Information A subset of PII that includes any health information that can identify an individual that is regulated by HIPAA, including full name, contact information, medical records, health insurance information, biometric elements, etc.; abbreviated as "PHI".
Pseudobulk A data transformation where counts from single cells are aggregated and considered in bulk, typically by summing counts across all cells of the same cell type within a sample (though counts can be averaged alternatively). Pseudobulks mirror how the data would look if that specific cell population had been isolated and sequenced using a traditional bulk experiment.
Quality Control The process of exploring and evaluating raw biological data to detect technical artifacts, errors, low quality data, biases, etc prior to downstream analysis in order to promote higher confidence results.
Quilt plot A type of plot that is used to visualize the two-dimensional spatial distribution of gene expression or cell types/clusters within a sample, mirroring the physical architecture of the tissue sample. Colors are used to represent the expression values or cell type/domain classes. Can be thought of as a type of heatmap without any clustering.
R-squared Statistical measure relating to the proportion of variability within the dependent varaible that can be explained by the independent variable and the relationship between the variables. Often used as a measure of effect size. Also known as the "coefficient of determination".
Random seed A number provided as an initial starting value for random number generators, promoting the repeatability and reproducibility of random processes.
Repeatability The ability for the same researcher to run the same analysis on the same data and get the same results.
Reproducible A term used to describe research or a process that a new researcher can follow, running the same analysis on the same data and getting the same results as the original researcher..
Resolution Refers to the smallest unit of change or observation scale that can reliably be detected or measured. High resolution means that a scale has fine intervals and small differences can be observed. Low resolution means that a scale has broader intervals and small variations may be missed (e.g., harder to differentiate between individual cells or measure all molecules).
RIN RNA Integrity Number. A measure of sample quality determined by an automated software algorithm. Found by analyzing the electrophoretic separation of RNA molecules according to size, processing the resulting signal traces (electropherograms which representing how much of RNA of different sizes is present in samples) to evaluate and rank the signal features and combinations of features according to their predictive information content. Returns a value representing the overal level of RNA degradation within a sample on a standardized 1-10 scale.
Schizophrenia Chronic neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by a spectrum of symptoms impacting a person's thoughts, emotions, and behavior. Symptoms are generally classified as positive, negative, or cognitive.
Segmentation Process of inferring individual cell boundaries from tissue images, nuclear or other cell surface and interior marker staining, and transcript coordinates. Some algorithms if only provided nuclear staining first segment nuclear boundaries and expand them to approximate the cell boundaries. Others use the combination of stains to first find cell boundaries. After inferring cell boundaries, observed transcripts can be assigned to cells.
Sequencing A laboratory technique that extracts and analyzes the genetic material from a sample.
Sequencing-based technologies Methods that utilize microscopic regions or spots on specialized slides to sequence intact tissues, retaining the original physical location of observed RNA molecules within a tissue sample. These methods have high molecular resolution but low cellular resolution because they sequence the whole transcriptome rather than targeting a limited set of genes. However, the spots are not necessarily single-cells and data must be deconvoluted.
Slope Coefficient within a linear model that represents both the steepness and direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variable.
Sparse Term used to describe the results from a single-cell or spatial transcriptomics experiment. Typically means that an experiment is low coverage, possibly because a given cell has a limited number of molecules that are being expressed at any given time. Within a data matrix, this looks like a lot of zeros. A sparse-matrix representation, on the other hand, omits these zeros, storing only the location of and values of non-zero data (though displays of this data may include periods instead of zeros if inspected). This sparse representation is used to reduce the overall storage size needed for the data.
Spatial Transcriptomics Spatial Transcriptomics refers to molecular methods used to measure gene expression at or near single-cell resolution, reporting where observed transcripts are physically located within the source tissue, preserving spatial context.
Specialized cell A highly differentiated cell that serves a specific purpose within a tissue. It may have a unique structure (physical shape or size) compared to other cells. It will express specific genes that other cells may not.
Spot Refers to the regions across a tissue section that sequencing-based methods use to capture spatially-resolved data. Spots often represent 10s to 100s of cells (e.g., multicellular) rather than single-cells. Each spot has a defined set of x- and y- coordinates on the slide used to capture data, relating captured data to physical locations within the tissue.
STlist An S4 object that the spatialGE program uses to store spatially-resolved expression data for one or multiple samples. Utilizes slots for various aspects of the data and named lists within each slot to store data specific to each sample.
Subcellular Represents part of a cell rather than a whole, single cell.
Transcriptome Complete collection of transcripts produced by the genome in a specific cell, tissue, or organism.
Vertex Where the endpoints of two edges meet in a polygon.
Violin plot A plot used to visualize distributions of numerical variables. Because they visualize the density of data across possible values of the variable, they may resemble violins. Useful for comparing the variable across different biological groups.
Visium A popular sequencing-based technology for generating spatially-resolved expression data.
Volcano plot A scatter plot that plots data points according to the observed magnitude of change on the x-axis and the statistical significance on the y-axis. Typically one point represents one gene, and the values are found by comparing the expression of that gene across different experimental conditions or clinical cases. Points are often colored differently based on whether the gene is upregulated (over-expressed) or downregulated (under-expressed) in a specific case relative to a conrol and whether the observed change reached pre-defined thresholds for statistical significance.
Xenium A popular image-based technology for geenrating spatially-resolved expression data.
